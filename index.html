FICO Field Form, ICT

The field form is built, and running on NIETE's sandbox WhatsApp number as /observe2live on sandbox. A coach fills it in while the lesson happens, seals it, sends the recording, then checks the moments found in it. Click through a whole visit below, or try it on a phone.

Try it on a phone

  1. Message the sandbox. From a coach account, send /observe2 to +92 302 5502255 ("Digital Coach Updates").
    Open the chat in WhatsApp
  2. Pick the teacher. School, teacher, then "Start observation", as in /observe. Then choose how long the period is.
  3. During the lesson. Start your phone's voice recorder app, not WhatsApp's. Open the form and fill it in as you watch: Part 1 until halfway, then Part 2.
  4. After the lesson. Stop the recorder and tap "Seal and send" in the form. Then share the recording to the chat. The moments to check arrive a few minutes later, and the brief after that.

The sandbox holds test accounts and test schools only. The number you message from needs a coach or school-leader account there; if the planner shows no school, add one from its menu. A sealed record can't be changed, by design, so practise on a practice lesson.

A whole visit, step by step

Everything in the phone comes from the code running on the sandbox, run here in your browser with a made-up lesson: the wording, the checks, the levels and the brief. The one thing not run is the model that finds the moments in the recording; a fixed answer stands in for it. Use Next, the arrow keys, or the buttons in the phone.

    StepWhat the server does
    
    
    /observe2Opens today's visit planner with a marker, so the Start that follows is known to be an /observe2 visit. Coaches and school leaders only; anyone else is told it is for coaches.

    Three ideas the rest depends on
    
  
    A. The recording counts what is said; the coach writes down what they see, when they see it
    
  
    A recording hears every question, every piece of praise and every wrong answer, with the minute it happened. A coach at the back of the room sees what no recorder can: how many different children spoke, whose hands were up, whether a group really worked together, whose hands were on the materials. So each side does what it is good at. During the lesson the coach answers plain questions about what they see, in two parts. Rumi finds the moments it heard and quotes them. Neither has to guess at the other's part.
    
  
    B. Seal before you look
    
  
    A coach who has seen Rumi's draft cannot un-see it. Today every coach starts from Rumi's draft. Most of it goes back as it arrived, and what does change mostly goes up: since mid-August the coach's final ends five points above Rumi on average. In Rawal Town two staff observers watching the same lessons went the other way, 81 changes down and none up, and Rumi's two drafts of those lessons disagreed with each other. The data can't say who is right, because none of it was collected independently. So the coach's record is locked, with the time, before anything from the recording is shown.
    
  
    C. Rumi finds moments; a fixed rule adds them up
    
  
    The new rubric makes level 3 something you can count: "at least two open questions, each answered by one child." So Rumi's job becomes finding and quoting those moments, a task anyone can check, rather than picking a level, the task on which the same lesson got different answers on different runs. The coach says yes or no to each moment, a fixed rule adds up the yeses, and the level arrives filled in. Rumi's own score is kept for the evals team and never shown to the coach.
    
  
    In one line
    The coach writes down what they see as it happens, Rumi says what it heard, the coach confirms, the rule adds up. Time is saved in the adding up, not by pre-filling what the coach saw.
    

    


    
  
    Nothing to memorise
    
  
    Seventeen indicators are too many to hold in mind while watching a class, and on 29 September Ahwaz settled that coaches don't have to: "They should just capture the evidence. That is their job while doing the observation. The rubric-based ratings come at the end, when all the evidence has been collected." So the form never names an indicator, a code or a level while the lesson is on. Its questions follow the lesson: how many children spoke, who got to answer, what happened when children worked, the books and materials, the switches between activities. The coach answers what they see, and the rule decides which indicator each answer feeds.
    
  
    The four moments from version 2 survive in one place: after the seal, Rumi groups what it heard under them, and the teacher's profile can use the same grouping. The pocket card is dropped, because a coach needs nothing in hand. The card below is for the team: it shows where each of the 17 sits.

    When the two parts differ
    
  
    Six rows are asked in both parts, so the rule needs to know which part counts when they differ. For who got to answer, group work, materials and children answering each other, the question is whether it happened at all, so the higher part counts. For switching activities, the question is whether the class settled each time, so the weaker switch counts. How many children spoke has its own rule: everyone from Part 1, plus those speaking for the first time in Part 2. These are our placeholders until Ahwaz's team decides (question 22).
    
  
    The holes
    
  
    A fixed rule has to give every count a level, and the rubric leaves some without one. Four rows (C1, C6, D1 and D4) have the same gap: level 2 describes "none of it", level 3 needs "at least two", and exactly one instance has nowhere to go. Take D1, depth of student reasoning. Level 2 is "every response restates what was given"; level 3 is "at least two students give a response showing reasoning". One child reasoning is neither. D3 has the same gap for a single exchange between children, and D2 never defines "spread across the lesson"; with two parts, our rule reads it as at least one child speaking in each. The rule scores the gaps 2 for now and marks them. That is a placeholder, not a decision (question 4). Ahwaz's principle from the call, "one level of evidence corresponds to one level in the rubric only", is exactly why each gap needs a level.
    

    


    
  
    The check and the debrief
    
  
    Ahwaz confirmed this half on 29 September as drafted: Rumi's moments with "yes, this happened" or "no, or not like this", then the coach's pick kept or changed with the first pick saved, then the debrief built from that. The debrief is the part the workbook left out and, Haroon told him, what worries coaches most: "how does this all feed into my debrief, because I've got to have that conversation with the teacher." We designed that link ourselves; Ahwaz's answer was "Sure, sure."
    
  
    Five design choices, and why
    
  
      
    
    1. One moment, one question: did this happen? Each moment is a quote with its minute, and the coach says yes or no. That is a question anyone can answer without training, and it gives the evals team exactly what they need: how often Rumi's moments are real.
      2. 
    
    2. Levels arrive filled in, from the coach's own evidence. After the yeses and noes, every level is pre-selected with what the coach's answers and confirmed moments add up to. This is not Rumi's rating, which stays hidden as the pilot plan asks. On 23 September the team put it this way: the pre-fill won't be on the evidence, but on which band the evidence lands in.
      3. 
    
    3. Plain words on every option. Instead of "3 · Proficient", the coach reads "Open questions, one child each". Each option is the rubric's own descriptor, shortened to fit WhatsApp's 30 characters.
      4. 
    
    4. One box for why, not seventeen. The server already knows which levels were changed and which moments were confirmed. One optional box at the end asks what changed the coach's mind.
      5. 
    
    5. Audio clips on the portal, later. A WhatsApp form cannot play audio. Each moment carries its minute and words; playing the clip from the observation's page on the NIETE portal is planned, not built.
      6. 
  
    
  
    What the teacher sees
    
  
    The workbook says: no section total, no overall score, no percentage. The sections measure different things, and a 1 to 4 scale can't be averaged meaningfully. On 25 September the product side agreed to move teacher reports to section percentages, and on 24 September the ICT team said heads and teachers keep asking for scores. Since 29 September, on NIETE's sandbox, every observation score a teacher or head sees is a band read off the percentage (Excellent, Good, Average, Below average, Needs support), and a head's STEPS view takes its ratings from coach visits only: S from Section F, T from Sections B and C pooled by points, E from Section D. Both assume a percentage exists, and the 17 have none. Our suggestion is a profile: each indicator's level in the same plain words, grouped by the four moments, with the priority and the one thing to try, and no number that adds rows together. What teachers and heads see, including how a profile becomes an S, T and E, is for Ali's and Asma's teams to decide with Ahwaz (question 12).

    How we got here
    
  
    This page assumes you were not in any of the earlier discussions. Twelve moments matter.
    
  
    
    
    WhenWhat happened
    
    
    27 Aug – 15 SepThe scoring-honesty work on today's FICO. On 2,788 live lessons scored on the current form (37 indicators, 148 marks), 93% of teachers landed in one 20-point band, and level 4 was given in 5% of judgements. Fourteen indicators barely move and hand every teacher about 26 percentage points whatever they do. A shorter 26-indicator version was built on sandbox. On 15 September Haroon kept the 37-indicator form in production and held the new one back.
    
    
    14 SepThe same lesson, different advice. The sandbox scorer scored the same 42 lessons three times each. Any two runs of the same lesson named a different "one thing to work on" 64% of the time.
    
    
    16 SepAhwaz's team opens the workbook "FICO ICT – Observation Framework": 17 indicators in three sections, an observation protocol, and a one-page field form. Taimoor, Qurat-ul-ain, Ali and Abdul Waheed commented on it between 16 and 24 September.
    
    
    18 SepThe "wobble". Sameer scored the same transcripts ten times over. On D2, eight of the ten runs came back split between level 1 and level 2.
    
    
    22 SepRawal Town. Two staff observers independently observed the same three teachers. Across 210 judgements they moved Rumi's pre-filled score down 81 times and up none. The two pre-filled forms, made from two recordings of the same lessons, disagreed with each other on 12 indicators. Across all coaches the direction is the opposite (see below).
    
    
    23 SepThe call this page follows from (Model Sovereignty + OSS, 83 minutes). Ahwaz presented the design: coaches record evidence on a blank form; Rumi scores the same lesson in the background; the pairs become the ground truth for testing models. Haroon took on turning the field form into a WhatsApp form, aiming for Monday 28 September.
    
    
    24 SepThe pilot plan. 21 teachers, 16 coaches, 50 lessons, about 12 weeks. In it, the coach's provisional scores lock before any output from Rumi, and Rumi's own scores are never shown to coaches.
    
    
    25 SepTwo meetings. With Qurat-ul-ain and Rifat, the protocol was flagged as too heavy for coaches. Agreed: the form coaches finally use must be streamlined, and teacher reports should show percentages. At the evals alignment, the 17 indicators were locked as the benchmark set, to be final by mid-October after the Islamabad pilot. Section B (lesson-plan fidelity) may come back.
    
    
    28 SepVersions 1 and 2 of this page. Version 1 turned the workbook's paper form into WhatsApp screens. Haroon found it hard to follow without training, so version 2 dropped the paper and asked eight plain questions after the lesson.
    29 SepHaroon and Ahwaz walk through version 2 (the last 20 minutes of an hour-long call). Settled: evidence is written down during the lesson, in two parts, with counts as numbers; no seating chart and no off-task sweeps; coaches aren't told which indicator they are scoring; everything after the seal stands as drafted. Version 3 of this page listed every change; notes on the call, with who said what, are in the project folder.
    30 SepVersion 3 of this page set out the design after the call: the form filled in during the lesson, in two parts, with counts as numbers.
    30 SepBuilt. The form, the check and the brief went live on NIETE's sandbox WhatsApp number as /observe2 the same day, with every question reworded in plain, observable words. The evals team's first coach-and-recording pairs come from the sandbox tests.
